Green Fee Commons Report · Toolkit Document 3

Public Accountability, Open Analysis & Responsible Use

For everyone: what this report is for, who reviews what, what anyone may do with the record, what no one may, what it never stores, and how to cite it.

Version 0.3 · 2026-10-12 · Āina Design Corp · supersedes Version 0.1 (August 2026, written against a fictional sample cohort)

Public record, not a State surface: every project in the Commons Report is a Green Fee appropriation enacted by the Legislature, listed from the State budget worksheet. Places, moku, and effects appear as programs report them; nothing here is a report from the State. No government adoption, endorsement, partnership, or deployment is claimed. The Commons Report supports professional review and public accountability; it makes no funding, eligibility, policy, or enforcement decisions.

1. What the Commons Report is for

Visitors to Hawaiʻi pay the Green Fee, and the Legislature funds stewardship with it. The Commons Report exists so that anyone can hold that spending to account and reason about it in the open: every enacted project listed in the moku it serves, every public notice about it recorded with its source, every figure a program reports traceable to who observed it, where, and when. Two uses follow, and this document exists to keep both honest:

  • Public accountability. A resident, a steward, a reporter, or a legislator can cite a record here — what was funded, for which moku, what has been reported since — and know exactly where each claim came from.
  • Open analysis. A researcher, a community group, or a program can take the published records as data: aggregate them by moku or department, compare across cohorts, join them to other public data, and publish what they find — provided the derivation is theirs and traces back to the record it was derived from.

The record is the unit of both. What makes it usable for either is the same thing: nothing publishes without passing a person, and nothing published is unsourced.

2. Human-review controls

Three gates stand between a registry edit and a public page, and a person holds each one:

  1. The record review — a reviewer decides when a record is published. The software checks that a summary, a story, at least one result, a source, and the moku are present, but it never publishes on its own. The budget facts and the public notices appear for every funded project without this review; the review is for the project's own story and its numbers.
  2. The change review — every edit to the register is recorded and reviewed, and the full history is public. A correction is a change anyone can read.
  3. The release switch — nothing goes live until a person turns publishing on for the site. Until then the checks run and the pages are built, but nothing is released.

The rule all three share: a person publishes, never software.

3. Permitted uses

  • Citing a record. Any enacted project, its appropriation, its place as reported, and any entry under As reported may be cited as public record, with the record identifier (GF-2027-HI-082) and the date or commit of the Commons Report consulted.
  • Aggregating and comparing. Sums, counts, and shares by moku, island, department, program area, or cohort; comparison across fiscal years as cohorts accrue; joins to other public data (the State's own layers, the Mokunet shared map, the research commons) by the record's identifier, moku, or program ID.
  • Deriving and publishing analysis. Indicators, rankings, maps, and models built on the published projection, published under the analyst's own name with the derivation stated and the source records named.
  • Reproducing the gates. Running the same validator and build on the same registry, or on a registry kept elsewhere, with the open toolkit the Commons Report is built on.
  • Professional review and adaptation of the architecture by the offices and programs that own real program records, under their own authority and review.

4. Prohibited uses

  • Representing the Commons Report as a government system or a State report, or implying agency adoption, endorsement, or partnership. Appropriations are the Legislature's; everything reported about them here is recorded from a named public source or from the program itself.
  • Using the Commons Report — or any real system built on this pattern — to make or justify funding, eligibility, zoning, staffing, enforcement, or permitting decisions. Screening views identify items for further professional review; they decide nothing.
  • Publishing derived, extrapolated, or modeled figures as if they were reported outcomes. A derivation is the analyst's and must say so; a reported outcome is the program's and carries its source note.
  • Filling in what the record marks to be reported — a place, a moku, an alignment — and presenting it as the record's. Where the Commons Report reads a place from an enacted title, it says so and asks for confirmation; an analysis that goes further must say the same.
  • Treating the fictional FY2026 demonstration cohort, kept as a validator fixture and marked sample wherever it builds, as real.

5. Information boundaries

  • In an enacted (fiscalPublic) cohort every record appears — in pages, feeds, and the machine-readable projection alike — with its public-law facts and its sourced reports. The program's story and its reported outcomes appear only once the record is published; until then the page says so, and the projection exports them empty.
  • Submitter contacts are accountability data, never public data: carried in the registry from validated onward, stripped from every projection, rendered on no page.
  • The registry carries no personal information beyond that, no financial system of record, and no pre-decisional program material; it must never be extended to do so. Parcel keys are references only; geometry, ownership, and taxation context stay with the county and State layers.
  • Stewardship narratives are the program's voice, released with their record; field observations aggregate before they publish.
  • The State's systems remain the authoritative source for every fact of the appropriation; disagreement is a registry defect by definition, corrected in a reviewed diff.

6. Versioning and release readiness

The schema, the toolkit documents, and the Commons Report carry explicit versions; release notes accompany every versioned release. The registry validates against schema v0.3, the stakeholder shape: sample is a declared cohort fact every record must match, so a fictional cohort says so on every page it builds and an enacted cohort never can; island, moku, and Act 96 alignment may be null until reported; a record cannot publish without its moku. Schema v1.0, the program-data profile (budget join keys, funding-release status, submitter accountability), is published alongside and exercised by fixtures. Every gate accepts a --registry location, so the identical rules run against data kept elsewhere.

Release readiness checklist: schema and invariants green in CI · publication gate exercised (unpublished stories and outcomes verified absent from pages and feeds) · provenance chain and As reported sources present on every page · public-record notice present on every page and export · accessibility checks passed · release note written.

7. Accessibility

The Commons Report targets WCAG 2.1 AA, consistent with the State of Hawaiʻi's EIT accessibility standards: semantic landmarks and headings, skip navigation, keyboard-visible focus, AA contrast, table captions and scoped headers, and no information conveyed by color alone. Accessibility findings are release blockers, not backlog items.

8. Open analysis on the published record

The Commons Report, its registry, its schema, and its pipeline are published as open, inspectable artifacts — accountability architecture that is itself accountable. For analysis, use the record, not the page:

  • The data surface is the machine-readable projection (registry/FY2027.published.json): one object per enacted record, with gated fields stripped unless published and contacts never present. Read from it rather than scraping pages; later cohorts publish beside it.
  • Identity is stable. A record's identifier is never reissued or renamed, and its route never moves once built. Cite the identifier, and the commit or date of the projection you read.
  • Provenance is on every record. The appropriation's worksheet page and program ID; each report's source and link; each outcome's source note; the registry commit that published it. Carry it forward: an analysis that cites a figure cites its source note.
  • Moku context follows the Mokunet shared map, the 33 moku of Hawaiʻi. It is regional context — whose place — never a land-use classification and never a jurisdiction; analyses by moku should say the same.
  • Corrections are welcome and public: open an issue on the repository naming the record and the source that contradicts it; a correction is a reviewed diff and the history keeps both versions.
  • Reproduce the gates with the open toolkit the Commons Report is built on, which runs the same validator and build on any registry and proves, on every change, that it still reproduces this one byte for byte.
