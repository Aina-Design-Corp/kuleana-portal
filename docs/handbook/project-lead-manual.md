# Project lead manual — one page per task

For the people who run a Green Fee project and the people who report on a
project's behalf. Every page has the same shape: *what this is for · what you
need · the steps · what happens next · who to ask*.

## 1. Your project on the report

**What this is for.** To know what the public page shows and where each
fact comes from.

Your project's page lists what the Legislature funded, what has been
publicly said about the project, and — once you have reported and a person
has approved it — your story and your results. Every fact on the page shows
where it came from. Your name and email never appear; they are kept for
follow-up only.

Until you report, the page says *to be reported* for the place and *not yet
published* for the story and results. That is honest, not a problem.

## 2. Reporting a cycle

**What this is for.** Once a month, or once a season, say what the project
did and what it observed. Thirty minutes is the target.

**What you need.** The form link the coordinator sent you (it opens already
set to your project), or the *Tell a project's story* issue template on the
repository; your field notes; any numbers the project already keeps.

**The steps.**

1. Confirm the project name at the top is yours.
2. **This period's work.** One or two sentences in your own words.
3. **Results.** For each thing the project measures, enter the number and
   the unit, the period it covers, and where the number comes from (a crew
   log, a count, a lab report). Leave blank what you do not have; blank is
   honest, zero is a claim.
4. **Observations.** What you saw that the numbers do not carry. Optional,
   and often the most useful part.
5. **Photo** (optional). One you took or have permission to share.
6. **Your name and email** stay with the record and never appear publicly.
7. **Submit.**

**What happens next.** Your report becomes a *draft*. The coordinator checks
it within about a week and emails you in plain words if anything is unclear,
naming the field. Once a reviewer approves, your page updates. Nothing
publishes without those two people.

**Who to ask.** The coordinator, by the address on the repository.

## 3. Reporting for a project that cannot use the form

**What this is for.** A crew without a form, a phone call, notes from a site
visit.

Enter the report yourself, on the project's behalf. Name yourself as the
submitter and the project lead or crew lead as the source of what you
entered. The record shows the source; it never shows you.

## 4. Adding a story or a photo

**What this is for.** The page's *In the project's own words* section.

Write it the way you would tell it to a neighbor. Say who is speaking. A
photo needs the consent of the people in it, and a line saying who took it.
*Approved for publication* means the project has agreed the words and the
photo may be public; the coordinator will ask if it is not clear.

## 5. Naming the place

**What this is for.** The island and the moku the project serves, and the
spot on the ground.

Give the island and the moku — the traditional district whose people the
work is for. If the report already shows one, read from the project's name,
confirm it or correct it. For the spot, a parcel number or a plain
description is enough; boundaries live in the county and State maps, not
here. A project's results count in its moku, or they do not count at all, so
the page cannot publish without this.

## 6. Recording a public notice

**What this is for.** Something was publicly said about the project — a
call for proposals, an award, a milestone, a news story.

Give the date, who said it, and a link. Notices go on the page as soon as
they are recorded; they do not wait for the monthly cycle.

## 7. What happens after you submit

`draft` — your report is in, visible to the coordinator only.
`validated` — checked; waiting for a reviewer.
`published` — a reviewer approved it; the page shows it.

A record moves one way, one step at a time, and a person makes each move.
Moving a published record back withdraws it from the page, and the history
keeps the reason.

## 8. If a published fact is wrong

Open a *Correction* issue on the repository, or tell the coordinator. The
fix is a reviewed change; the old value stays in the history with your issue
linked to it. Nothing is silently changed.

## 9. Who to ask

The coordinator first. The repository's Issues tab for anything in writing.
For the practice itself — how to observe, count, and record — Moku Steward
Training at Hui Koʻe ʻĀina.
